What I learned

Working with the team charter helped me understand that a project needs more than a product idea. It needs a shared purpose, clear responsibilities, agreed communication methods and working principles. ASSESSMENT AREA 02 · 20 MARKS

User Stories & Tasks

User stories describe a need from the perspective of a person who will use or be affected by the product. Tasks help the team plan the work needed to deliver each story.

EVIDENCE

Turning requirements into manageable work

The NavigSA team used Trello to organise user stories and tasks. The reflection records stories for five user groups: international students, French- or Portuguese-speaking students, service providers, administrators and the project supervisor. It also discusses priority labels and the breakdown of larger items into smaller sprint cards.

The accompanying Product Backlog provides the later, expanded set of stories and task details. The dated versions are kept visible rather than presented as if they were created at the same time.

Open user stories reflection ↗Open supporting backlog
PERSONAL REFLECTION

What I learned

Working with user stories helped me see why requirements should be described from the user's point of view. The benefit in a story—often expressed through the words “so that”—keeps the team focused on the problem the feature is intended to solve, rather than only on the technical task.

The review also identified areas that could be improved. Stories need clear acceptance criteria, tasks should be visible as checklists, and large items should be divided into manageable work. I also learned that Trello is only useful as evidence when its titles, descriptions and statuses remain accurate. In future work, I would define acceptance criteria before a story enters a sprint and review the board at the end of each sprint week.

PERSONAL REFLECTION

What I learned

Developing and reviewing the Product Backlog helped me understand that it should be more than a simple list of activities. It should connect the needs of the intended users with the work required to deliver the product. Clear user stories, priorities, task breakdowns and assigned responsibilities make the project's requirements easier to understand and manage.

I also learned that a backlog must be maintained as the project changes. New information, feedback and development decisions can affect priorities and scope. Keeping the Product Backlog connected to the Sprint Backlog helps the team understand why each task exists and how it contributes to the overall objective.

PERSONAL REFLECTION

What I learned

The Sprint Backlog helped me understand how a larger project can be divided into smaller, time-focused pieces of work. Linking sprint tasks to Product Backlog items makes it easier to see the purpose of each activity and how it contributes to NavigSA.

PERSONAL REFLECTION

What I learned

Maintaining the Agile Logbook helped me see the importance of recording project activity consistently. The entries show that planning, discussion, feedback and resolving blockers are part of development, even when no code is being written.

During the week of 28–31 July, presenting the Figma prototype helped the team identify that parts of the Product Backlog needed to be restructured. We acted on that feedback, refined the stories, prioritised the work and prepared the Sprint Backlog. I learned that feedback is more valuable when it leads to a visible change in the project record.

I also learned to document impediments rather than ignore them. Waiting for team confirmation and estimates can delay progress even when the work is otherwise prepared. In future projects, I will keep records aligned with the task board, communicate blockers early and make sure planned work is realistic.
